### *where am I?*

*I am in the depths of the dark fertile sea, bubbling up insights from a fecund subsurface seaweed raft. At times the slimy vines spasm and squeeze me. Pain, pure and alive, ripples through the trance. Creatures squirm in my guts. To them I say, hello, can I sing you a song? The brine muffles my voice. 

I am hollowing out, stretching, softening. The threshold of motherhood approaches. When I cross I will take my son in my arms. I will look in his eyes. We will be two but still one, for now we are one but still two. Words fail here. 

There is something happening to my brain and spirit as my belly grows. Crossing the threshold past maidenhood is a strange and scary thing. It is entering a marshy forest. I am lucky to drive a hundred hours to hold for a few minutes the hands of my mother and grandmother. They welcome me and light my way. 

Perhaps at other times I would have been familiar with the clearings in the thicket where wise women gathered. In this place and age they seem to be missing and mangled or dead. Some beckon through the portal of a screen. But I am on a screen enough. I want instead the unfiltered light in my sisters' eyes. 

A land mate, a mother too, asked me kindly, *how are you?*

*My body is fine, just fine. My heart is heavy. I am in a long process of grieving.*

She nodded.

-- 

Becoming a mother means Death, means Grief. Large parts of my brain turn off, orienting me towards a new function. I am lying in the sand at the bottom of the sea being rewired. 

I can only remember like a dream what it was like to have those parts of my brain. I only remember having my balance and my body functions. Being able to jump a fence or fit in a bathtub. The urge to roam.

There is plenty to grieve. Selfishly for my own maidenhood. (That one stage of life which our culture hold above all else, the golden crown for women - why would I give it up, voluntarily, on purpose?) 

For the loss of my coveted independence. For my body how it was.

In the space hollowed out by the waters of grief comes joy. 

Joy in the beauty of my belly and breasts, ripe. Celebration of my expanded awareness of care. Settling into the rhythms of family after years of wandering, I don't miss that stage anymore. I am happy and cocooned in the arms of my husband, in the walls of our miracle cottage, on this land by the forest. 

Our bubble is too good to be true. Sometimes the guilt sets in. I hear that outside the world is burning. Somewhere else there is war and plague.

How can I enjoy myself when--

But how can I not?

My new grandmother-in-law, a vibrant and sharp woman in her eighties, climbed a mountain to our wedding and delivered sage advice. 

*Make a store of the good times in your heart, so you can draw upon it in the hard times. Because times will be hard.*

### *where are we?*

The world burns and crumbles, ruins of empire on top of the graveyard of Eden. New robotic gods step out of the ashes and their reign looms imminent. 

Outside in the city, in the castles of "my people," there are no children. Many decide to end their bloodlines because to continue would be a hassle. How many spaces will I be excluded from because I chose a beautiful hassle? 

I have held maybe one baby in my life, my own child will be the first I learn to care for. And that's not even abnormal.

How'd we get here? Is this how we really want to live?

"*if enough people believe in an absurd world, absurd but consequential histories will unfold*" - Venkatesh Rao

Although we might pretend otherwise, no one person makes history move. Needs bubble up in culture, groups hijack and redirect them. 

History moves as a tide linked to forces we can't fully understand. We ride its swells. Sometimes a great person or idea or event crests one of those waves and we attribute the change to them personally. There's always more to the story. Who made them?

Truly, the interesting part is beneath the surface: in the powerful pushing force of history's wave that offers its white-capped crest to the sky. The inexorable advancement of its waters which are destined to break on the shore, transforming-terraforming. Each wave-break leaves a new beach, new cracks in the stone of the earth, new soft sands. 

--

Oh, this is all a very beautiful (or mediocre, or terrible, or boring) poem. But it's not very practical. What am I getting at?

I will speak with a language more concrete: that of the movies. 

### *pink tide crashing

In July the highly anticipated films Barbie and Oppenheimer were released on the same day: stories about the title character, each one a swirling crackling crest of a wave of history. *Barbie* tracks dynamics of the imaginal world where, through the construct of Barbie, women are everything; *Oppenheimer* tells the tale of the beginning of the atomic age. 

Their synchronous release had become, for some, an omen of sorts. A commentary by some unknown divinity about the last century of American culture. A delightful mystic-intellectual mystery. I answered the call gladly with popcorn, journal, and pink drink in hand. 

I saw Barbie the same weekend that Taylor Swift played two shows in my hometown Seattle. The concert and film combo had flooded the city's streets with mothers and daughters covered in pink sequins. These women and girls were there to consume media that was by them, for them. Not possible 10, 20, 50 years ago. That's a small ripple of time on the ocean of history.

It was uncanny, awe-some, beautiful. It was a wave of history crashing with a shimmer and giggle on the shore. SSSssssss. We have done it: women can make media for themselves, consume media made for us, make the money to buy the tickets and the outfits and pay for it from their own bank accounts. A decade ago the shore was a different shape. Now the wave has broken the rocks into a new shiny valley. Now we live in a new and uncharted World. 

### *real worlds

I am enjoying playing with the definition of World that Venkatesh Rao puts forth in his sensemaking writings from Ribbonfarm Studios. [Link]

In his definition, Worlds are answers to the question, "what sort of world do we live in?" Worlds are values-based constructs we inhabit. Worlds are made up of a pair of realities: how things are and how they ought to be. 

For example, that women aren't able to occupy positions of power and ownership, and that they ought to. 

On the whole, Rao argues, most worlds are "butterflies flapping in the chaotic weather-systems of history, hoping to cause hurricanes." There are, he claims, perhaps about a few hundred worlds that are significant. The "real world" is the amalgam of the dozen or so worlds that visibly matter. 

I seriously doubt Rao's ability to quantify something as unknowable as the number of worlds. But I agree on the general gist. Humans live in many life-defining Worlds. But it is the big, shared Worlds which move the tides of history. 

I call the way these Real Worlds weave together: "Culture."

I love the word Culture because it provides the useful image of humanity as bacteria in a petri dish. Each Real World is a strain of bacteria. As a superorganism we should strive for a symbiotic scoby / macrobiome to attain the benefits of optimal gut health. Unfortunately the cultures readily available from my vantage point are full of parasites and lacking healthy good bacteria. Much like the flora in the guts of people living in popular cultures. 

Humanity isn't a poison. Some of its cultures and Worlds, however, are poisonous. And these Worlds are very, very real. They have already destroyed a lot. 

### *a tangent about Barbieland*

Barbieland is a World that visibly impacts the real world. It is an imaginal realm where girls dream of becoming as-before-unimagined things, like girl doctors and girl astronauts and girl presidents. This creates movement in the Real World. Those inspired little girls go after their dream house, medical school, a doctorate, the presidency. Those girls become something they wouldn't have without the existence of Barbieland. 

In *Barbie*, the viewer is flung into a deteriorating Barbieland. Stereotypical Barbie, (played deftly by Margot Robbie), has begun to wonder about death, along with other disturbing symptoms of the incursion of reality. She is pushed reluctantly onto her initiatory heroine's journey to the real world.

It turns out that America Ferreria's character Gloria was dreaming into existence the "defects." Gloria, the mother of a teenage girl, has been revisiting her childhood Barbie doll, using Barbie as a creative outlet for the depression and anguish she's experiencing as she ages. Her drawings of bastard Barbies like Thoughts of Death Barbie and Cellulite Barbie enter Barbieland like all other daydreams of the toy. 

Ultimately America's Gloria is the heroine of the film, saving Barbieland from the viral meme of patriarchy. (*Meme*: a thought-snippet). 

Gloria's rousing speech about the conundrums of a woman under Patriarchy is the vaccine, freeing the Barbies still under Ken's spell. The women-dolls take back their world and their parliament. Even Gloria's moody daughter is dressed in frills, smiling emptily. 

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V2I6sBa2d-c]

After this experience, Robbie's Barbie can't stay in Barbieland. She's been contaminated by the Real. Now, Barbie wears a simple modest sundress with notably imperfect seaming and a soft and open expression. The empty doll-grin from before has been broken by existential crises plus her trials and tribulations. 

The costuming in this film is impeccable, the dress's seaming is no accident. The choice of a sundress one could buy at a department store for $50 is the perfect costume for Ordinary Barbie. 

Robbie's Barbie chooses to come back to the real world and become a woman, tempted by the real-rawness of it, symbolized by the montage that plays of scenes from the cycle of motherhood. 

She slips on pink Birkenstocks, her feet flat for good. The film ends with one more initiation: her first trip to the gynecologist. 

--

Pregnancy, motherhood, and birth are essential to the storyline of Barbie. The tensions of motherhood are the central conflict of for the main heroine, Gloria. But pregnancy itself is consistently sidelined for comedic effect. 

The film says something real from the Maiden's perspective. But that transition into Mother is, while present and mystic and looming, treated with fear and unconscious disgust. It is just out of reach. No surprise that Greta Gerwig, having never become a mother, can't talk about it. 

During the movie when the real pregnant Barbie, Midge, was introduced, the women around me smiled and pointed at me. Ha! It's you! In the film Midge is a punchline. My presence was too. 

*Who wants a pregnant Barbie?* The film keeps Midge hidden away for comedic effect, with a mild disgust. Pregnant Midge appears for her shock value, because she's out of place. *Who wants a pregnant Barbie?* 

Barbieland is a fantasy of women owning the men's world - Pregnancy does not fit.

In this world, women enter the workforce previously occupied by men and bedazzle it. 

Many women who have achieved success in the modern working world find themselves at a loss for what to do when they realize they want to become mothers. Their peers only understand the values of the maiden-playing-man. If there is a mother of children she is stuck at the front desk, she has outsourced her family, she is at best part-time. 

Maybe there is a mother of a company, a womanboss, but the creation of life occurs elsewhere, away somewhere. Keep the kids out of here. This here, the center of it all, is for our minds and our stuff and our commerce. 

So the women continue alone at home. Walled up reading in their gardens. Walking with their strollers. Many these days lament about the cost of childcare - the cost of outsourcing care of the family to the market. Some demand the state subsidize this cost. 

Where are the grandmothers, grandfathers, uncles and aunties? Where is the garden children can be booted out to roam? The world is not more dangerous than it once was. Children don't need more care. We are simply more scared. We are simply more alone. 

I watched Cheaper By The Dozen recently, a movie about a lovingly chaotic family of 14 strained by each parent's desire and opportunity to leave the home and fulfil their personal dream. When they need it most, there is no one to care for their dozen children for a few hours or a week. No grandmother or grandfather or sister or brother or friend. Why?

I guess it's American to do it all by yourself - even when it's messy and painful and sad. Now that's success. 

### *so, stop and start again*

Things are shifting now. More and more people can work from home, where the children live. A five-day office workweek is dead. Some call it the birth of the Creator Economy. Some call it late stage capitalism.  [https://www.nytimes.com/2023/10/16/opinion/office-work-home-remote.html#:~:text=Hybrid%20work%20arrangements%20have%20killed,and%20costs%20of%20a%20commute.]

The world I was brought up in is crumbling, splintering. But the new world isn't born yet. We are birthing it together. 

There is a baby in my belly, a whole new life - new world who can breathe and see and hear and think a little bit. I often forget this, caught up in my own dramas. Because I still contain him, he is not ready yet. He is still waiting to be browned around the edges. 

Months ago he began making slight stirrings. Flutters and hiccups, just between us. Now he makes tectonic twisting movements, leg and back and hand. My belly moves enough that my neighbor balks at the squirm.

There is no wonder anymore, no guessing, this being is coming and he will be born soon. 

It is like this with the birth of a new World too. Emerging through many people are fragments and stirrings of a new way of life. For some of us this World is bursting from our bellies, maybe for some of us this World is already born to ourselves. But it is on the whole not yet real, not yet united. 

We can make it real. We can foment-ferment a new culture. Gravity pulls our pieces together and sometime soon we will birth a new World... but it is not born yet, it does not have a name. It is easy to forget. It is still inside our bellies. 

### *it's happening*

Remember. It's happening. The stirrings and hiccups, tectonic spinal movements in your belly - others feel it too. A new World is being born through us. It's normal to crumble and melt before birth. It's normal, it's needed, it's necessary. 

The midwife says, it's because all your brains are in your bottom. You need them there. Your body is opening up to push out a new being. It's normal, it's normal, it will make sense later. For now, relax, be here. Scream and cry if you have to. Ask for help and company. 

Soon we will have something new, something alive and perfect and so so beautiful. Something we can't quite imagine from here, where we are still crumbling.
