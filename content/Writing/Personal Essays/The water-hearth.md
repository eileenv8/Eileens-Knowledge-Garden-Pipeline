## Spiritual Hydration
One Sunday a few years ago I attended a Methodist service in Port Angeles. The pastor Ken, a sharp and attractive man with silver hair, preached about water being the ordinary stuff of life, like food. "When we don't drink enough water, we get dehydrated. It's the same thing with Spirit -- we get spiritually dehydrated. There's an epidemic of spiritual dehydration." 

Then he called up some volunteers from the audience to illustrate a legend. 

[Image of vessels pouring into a central container]

5 volunteers stood around the baptismal font holding various vessels: little plastic holy water containers, small vases, a glass. As he poured water into each vessel from a little clay jug, the pastor indicated for the volunteers to do the same. He told a story about some chiefs of tribes somewhere doing the same -- but I've now forgotten this story.

"Don't pour all of it - keep some for yourself. Yes, like that."

"That's the community," he said, pointing to the bowl that each person had poured. "Now, try and take your water out."

Quiet laughter murmured through the church.

"That's right, you can't! It's impossible to take your water out. Once you're in, you're in." 

Then, he takes a small jug and pours water into the center container. "That's what the lord does." 

We watch as the central container fills far beyond what could be achieved with individual contributions. 

My research and aspirations center around this: coming together to create something more than each of us. The right kind of collaboration, the right kind of being-together, makes 1+1=3. It works, I can't explain it.

This part, "you can't take your water out," was new. I was struck with a familiar pain: the pain of missing all the places I've left. I love to pour my "spiritual waters," my attention and love, into the people around me. Then, part of me is left there. 

"How do you know if you're spiritually dehydrated? Look at your relationships," Intoned the pastor. 

At risk of pointless self-flaggelation, I reluctantly looked at the reality of my own spiritual dehydration. 

I can talk circles around most spiritual philosophies, debate metaphysics for days, share unique and interesting insights from my own experiences of ecstasy...but I struggle to maintain relationships. In pursuit of my lofty goals I spiritually bypass the sticky reality of being a human. I am still to an extent deluded by the fantasy that I, a young woman in her early 20's, can figure out the world and clutch this figured-out-world held to my chest, unshared except in cryptic tidbits. 

[bag full of cryptic tidbits]

The reality is, I am chronically transient and have a long history of leaving relationships along with places. I attended 6+ different schools in my youth and left the relationships I'd cultivated to die in each place. Mostly.

### The roots of humans
A question I live with is: what are the roots of humans? We are not trees, intimately entwined with place through one patch of soil. It is not just the food we eat and the air we breathe, although that's a part of it. We need heart-nourishment, each of us, or we will wither and die. We need spiritual hydration. 

Some people choose to be physically rooted by attaching themselves to things and places and people - but it seems that others live lightweight, finding food and water and love all over the world. They are rooted in the moment, rooted in relationship, rooted simply to the earth. This is the nomadic dream. 

Does it really work? It didn't for me the way I was doing it. I've wound up okay, falling in love with a person in a place, but wishing for a proper squad. The things I want to create take time.

I am experimenting with some places I return to again and again to pour in the waters of my heart. We're calling it our 'migration spiral'. 

like Montaia, a retreat center in the Sierras that I found through my work with Cabin DAO. First, for two weeks as a maiden, I found short-term community there. I returned back for a month, pregnant and partnered, and we were married in the grove of the Patriarchs (some of the oldest trees in the world). This summer I will return again. 

This summer I also returned again to San Francisco, a city of great expansion for me, and I'm finding new breadths on which to stretch myself. 

I miss Elkenmist, the community farm that held us in the tenderest of times, as my son and I were born into young mombabyhood. 

I miss Orcas Island, the depth of its nature and people, the soft mosses and wild forests and remoteness as a feature.

I miss...

I left the waters of my spirit in each time and place, and didn't return to the spiritual hydration of the people I loved. I turned my back and froze my grief. 


A teacher of mine once said, 
### *grief is rocket fuel for groundlessness.*

We are all falling through time, subject to uncertainty. You can try to cling to the vines on the sides of the bottomless pit, you can scream as you fall, but the easiest way (the way without suffering) is to learn to work with the wind and fly. 

And, in life, we fall in love. We find things to hold on to. Then death happens, leaving happens. We let go and fall again - we grieve and release.

Sadness is this power, the power of connection and healing and release. 

Inside me there is a cabinet full of the griefs of leaving, over and over. It turns out, without grief, I never quite got my reserves back - the waters of my sadness remained there, frozen in time and place. I never released the things I loved to make space for something new. 

I try to go back, and back again. Get what I had before. Sometimes it is real, to return to a place I had rooted, sometimes it is a mirage. 


As a native to the Pacific Northwest, I’m blissfully unaqcuainted with drought. I remember in college, my Californian friend and I joked about the moral politics of long showers - she still felt guilty to take them, even in water-rich Portland OR. Her years in LA County had instilled in her a moral imperative to conserve the scarce resource. Drought is chronic in LA. Long showers are wrong. But as a well-hydrated Seattleite, I grew up amid the clear lakes scattered around the mountains that rise out of Puget Sound. I wasn't taught to linger over a bath or a long shower - unless I was hogging the bathroom. Water was everywhere - we had too much - and I took it for granted. 

Last October I lived in a desert in drought: rural Johnson City Texas, outside of Austin. The water out of the tap was heavy and ominously flavorful, the deep caverns of groundwater giving their reserves reluctantly. 

While I was there, I needed to cry, (like anyone who feels things does). I went down to the river for the first time. I confronted a dry riverbed. My emotions swirling and tears welling up, I wanted to be closer to the heart of the earth, close to the water. I climbed down the banks to lie face up in the dry creekbed, wrapped in the pungent smells of the bacterial life that hung onto the crunchy riverbed and lingered in tiny fecund ponds. When the wave of sadness passed I sat under a nearby tree and played my instrument, singing for rain. I like to think the earth sang through me.

Some time later, we had a thunderstorm. Ecstatic, celebrating with the dry earth, I skip-hiked in my uggs through the deluge. I waded up a hill towards the house that held my friends, through the new river that had found its way over our footpaths when the hard soil couldn't drink the rains fast enough. When I get there, drenched and grinning, we were complete. Everyone together. The storm wrapped around us, we drank tea and laughed. We warmed our hands together. 

Once, I asked a friend who'd lived at Tamera Ecovillage: *what did you learn there about building community?*
She replied, *it starts with the water.*
At Tamera, that's where they started. A group of visionaries chose a piece of land in arid Portugal, and set about first building water catchment systems.

In March I attended EthDenver, a chaotic and buzzing gathering of tens of thousands of folks plugged into the Ethereum ecosystem (If you don't know, Ethereum is like Bitcoin but better). Down in the bowels of the massive conference center that contains it, I witnessed coordination issues and felt the pain of corporate insanity poisoning even the water that we drink. Flows of water are a great place to see signs about what's going on in your community. 

I attended to seek out new collaborators and enjoy time with internet friends, so I made few plans and decided to rely on serendipity (a favorite strategy of mine). Excited about the opportunity to serve, I decided to volunteer. I chose team food & beverage. I find fulfillment in serving food and drink, being the one who gives nourishment to people’s bodies. Often you’ll find me handing out chocolates or gummies just to spread the love. (No, not “fun” gummies). I fantasized about pouring tea and delivering hot lunches to hungry people during the busy event.

The reality was bleak. Due to coordination issues, three of us lingered in the warehouse beneath the conference, frustrated by the pointlessness of our directives, a lack of direction, and an imposed impotence to solve the real problems at hand. Three of us, two staff and I, delivered a cart of snacks to a room full of people who commented snarkily on how they didn’t really need them. And we couldn't serve people with the one thing they did need: water. 

My supervisor received frantic messages about a lack of water access - besides scarce water fountains, there was no access to water other than buying small thick plastic bottles of Nestle-owned Aquafina, a limitation imposed by the rules of the Pepsi-owned venue. Plus, the venue had promised to provide water stations and flaked. Now some of the staff was in a panic about it, and some of the staff was disgruntled about their panic. 

Possibilities were tossed around. I suggested we fill the plastic bottles we had on hand and distribute them to thirsty staff. One of the staff lamented, *I'd love to just go buy a case of water bottles, but who's going to pay for it? Not me!* 

Eventually, a woman with higher-up discretion came down with an agentic, tentative directive for my immediate supervisor: *take this card, and go to the store and buy a bunch of water. But make sure it's exactly the right kind - the small bottles of Aquafina.* Witnessing this, I was painfully sad. The venue had failed to provide as promised, yet still the invisible threat of retaliation by the venue's corporate overlords imposed arbitrary restrictions upon the hardworking staff's ability to obtain high-quality water while minimizing waste. To me, Aquafina was the worst of the worst: thick plastic bottles housing machine-processed tap water. It'll hydrate you, of course. But from my witchy nit-picky perspective, of possible drinkable waters it's about as far away from life-filled springwater as you can get. It would be easy and possible to buy bottles or jugs of springwater at less cost from the same store - but it wouldn't happen. 

Around this time, I saw a group of women unboxing a brand-new water-filling station, looking with despair upon its complexity. I wanted to help, but decided my help wasn't helpful: they had enough capable bodies and minds on the problem. At this point the system that had brought them to this point would play out and I was just an observer.

With no tasks available to do and pain about the Aquafina situation working in my heart, I decided to leave early. I'd spotted an internet aquaintance in the beanbag-dancefloor room on our last delivery trip, and I was itching to ask him to join me on the empty dancefloor. So I left with some honest parting words. *I'm useless here right now. This is painful. Text me anytime if you need extra hands.* Then, I ventured back up into the upper guts of the Conference-Festival. 

The dancefloor was ominously empty despite the fantastic DJ. Past the open area next to the booth was a spattering of beanbags filled with people on their phones, extending deep into the shadowy hall. Loud danceable electronic music pulsed through the space, permeating into the beanbag rest area and nearby walking paths. For the nearby areas, it was just a bit too loud for talking walking or resting -- but in the open area the volume was perfect for dancing. 

I knew my acquaintance would be down to dance - I'd watched him via twitter fall in love with my former home and beloved community space, The Center SF. The Center is a yogic tea temple, and it would be hard to find a volunteer there who wouldn't be down for a bit of a midday jive. So I introduced myself, pulled him up from his beanbag, and we danced. We threw energy around. It was so, so fun, being together.

I tried to rope-in passersby, but no one, even not that guy in a festival tiger onesie, gave in. When I was finished dancing and trying in vain to get a bigger party started, I sat down to chat with my new friend and noticed again how abrasively loud the music was. Why were so many people tolerating this? I tried to troubleshoot, asking the DJ to turn it down a bit. *Your music is great, but it would be a lot more pleasant as background music if it were a bit quieter*. 

It didn't work - he turned it down maybe a smidge. I gave up, opting to endure. 

In comparison with the water crisis occuring behind the scenes, I noticed an imbalance in the feminine aspect of the festival. The feminine is the force of mystery and dissolution, and the dark feminine might dissolve boundaries that are needed for clarity. The music was doing this, dissolving the boundaries between three potentially clear and useful spaces and destroying all three of them: seeping into the resting space making it difficult to use for connection or rest, and the sight of a sad empty dancefloor hitting the eyes of passersby in a decidedly non-harmonious manner. 

But the bright nourishing waters of the feminine hadn't quite broken through. People were still thirsty.
