The wind carries the metal body of our jeep over rolling hills. It's the height of summer in California, north of the San Francisco Bay. We are soaring through amber waves of grain. Our thoughts undulate with the rolling of the hills.

"It's Steinbeck country," Calvin says, again. His romantic view of these oaken hills opens a portal for me to walk through, dredging back up my sight from its focus on my own neuroses. I was, having lived there, acclimated to the sights of paradise.  

When we were still pretending we could buy land, we oggled over an old, old homestead for sale. The gorgeously crafted cabin with a small orchard nestled in a jagged crook of the golden oaken hills. Priced around 100k, it was in need of new settlers. That frontier is still remote, still dangerous. Like our ancestors who were called to America seeking harsh new beginnings, the desert called us too. 

As a teenager struggling with the soured nest of a broken family, Calvin was deeply touched by Steinbeck's novel. To him, the novel said: sometimes, our parents and ancestors are just bad people. What can we do? Steinbeck's refrain is, *thou Mayest.*

Thou mayest transcend your inheritance. Thou Mayest become something new. 

We are inextricably linked to our ancestors, but we are not them. In fact, it's our prerogative to take what we'd like to and discard - or compost - the rest. 

*Culture has always been carried on through composting, not preserving. It's a new and Western idea to romanticize things and protect them as in a museum. - Dougald Hine, Doomer Optimism #128

It's very western to spurn the compost. We spurn it even for our own bodies. Embalm, preserve, put away.
